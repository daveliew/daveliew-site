## The raw material

There's a parenting lens and learnings about how to use AI as a companion to
learning, but also guarding them at certain instances.

My daughter was very curious because in school she learned that China is 4,000
years old and that Iran and Egypt are the oldest countries in the world, but
she wasn't sure of their ages. First, we took turns to guess how old they are.
She guessed 7 million, and I said, "Oh, maybe 7,000 is a closer number." We
also asked her simple math, like if something is older, would it be a bigger or
smaller number than China's 4,000? She clearly intuited more and therefore
landed on 7,000 as a guesstimate age of Egypt and Iran.

We then unveiled a bit of world history by exploring how countries have stayed,
and we also traced back to how Russia is so big or why it always fights so
often. We also explored how Mongolia used to be such a huge empire but waned in
size due to various reasons. We really unlocked a nice exploration of various
themes of geography and economics. It was grounded by her basic understanding
of humans, which is that humans need water, oxygen, and food to survive. We
understood how areas such as middle central Africa are landlocked and therefore
had to pay more to obtain food and water. At the same time, in terms of selling
and being able to generate money, they weren't as lucky to get access to
educational resources to sell, and therefore they were at a net poorer state
than other countries around them.

This was an interesting and fascinating journey of using AI to enhance our
experience by being the kid and being child-led, but with the parent also
guiding and steering some of the thinking and implanting concepts in them.

## Broader frame Dave described (context for the essay)

Teaching the kids multiple subjects — geography, history, economics, transfer
of value and means of trading — as concepts for understanding the state of
people and the world, and helping to shape them as future contributors.
