My daughter came home from school with a fact and a question. She'd learned
that China is 4,000 years old, and that Iran and Egypt are the oldest
countries in the world — but she wasn't sure how old they were.

We took turns to guess. She opened with 7 million. I offered that maybe 7,000
was a closer number. Then we asked her some simple math: if something is older
than China, would its number be bigger or smaller than 4,000? She clearly
intuited more, and landed on 7,000 as her guesstimate for Egypt and Iran.

From there the thread kept pulling. We unveiled a bit of world history by
exploring how some countries have lasted. We traced why Russia is so big and
why it has fought so often. We looked at how Mongolia was once a huge empire
and waned over time. And we grounded all of it in her basic understanding of
humans: people need water, oxygen and food to survive. That model took us to
landlocked middle Africa — paying more to obtain food and water, with less
access to educational resources to sell, and therefore net poorer than the
countries around them.

It turned into a genuinely fascinating exploration of geography and economics,
none of it planned. The AI's job was to be the kid alongside her — following
her curiosity, answering at her level, staying child-led. Mine was to guide
and steer some of the thinking: choosing the next question, planting a concept
where it would take, and knowing when to step in.

No lesson plan would have routed from "how old is Egypt?" to trade access in
landlocked countries. The question-asking was the curriculum.
