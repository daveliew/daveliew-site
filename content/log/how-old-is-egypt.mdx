My daughter came home from school with a fact and a question. She'd learned
that China is 4,000 years old, and that Iran and Egypt are the oldest
countries in the world — but she wasn't sure how old they were.

What followed turned into an unplanned tour of geography and economics. The
interesting part, looking back, is the shape of it: who spoke when, and where
the AI was allowed in.

## The transcript, annotated

  </Turn>

## The moves

1. **Guess before you look.** A committed guess turns the answer into a
   payoff instead of a fact to absorb.
2. **Calibrate by ordering, not telling.** "Bigger or smaller than 4,000?" is
   a question she can win with reasoning she already has.
3. **Ground in invariants.** Every new fact got tested against what she
   already trusts: people need water, oxygen and food.
4. **Pull the thread.** One answered question buys the next: Russia →
   Mongolia → landlocked Africa.

## Where the AI was allowed in — and where it wasn't

The AI never answered first. The guessing game was ours: commitment,
calibration, her landing on 7,000 — no lookup. Only after she owned a number
did we ask the AI together, and its job then was to be the kid alongside
her — following her curiosity, answering at her level, staying child-led.
Mine stayed steering: choosing the next question, planting a concept where it
would take, and knowing when to step in.

An answer before the guess would have killed the game. That's the boundary.

No lesson plan would have routed from "how old is Egypt?" to trade access in
landlocked countries. The question-asking was the curriculum.
